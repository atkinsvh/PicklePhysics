## The picture, in one paragraph

A plant is a substrate-organized structure. The plant's cells are synchronization domains in the substrate. The plant's essential oils are low-molecular-weight substrate vortices that the plant stores in specialized glands — oil cells, glandular trichomes, secretory canals, resin ducts. The plant makes these molecules as a *currency*: the oils are how the plant communicates with insects, defends against pathogens, attracts pollinators, regulates its own temperature, and signals stress. The oils are the plant's vocabulary.

When you harvest the plant, the oils are still in the glands. To get them out, you have to do one of three things:

1. **Disrupt the glands mechanically** (cold pressing, grinding, chewing). The oils are released directly. The simplest method. The cheapest. The one that requires no heat and no solvent.

2. **Provide enough thermal energy to vaporize the oils** and then condense the vapor (steam distillation, hydrodistillation, solar distillation). The oils are carried over with water vapor. They separate from the water because they are not miscible with it. The standard industrial method. The one that works for most aromatic plants.

3. **Provide a solvent that the oils prefer to the plant's cellulose and lignin network** (maceration in oil, ethanol, vinegar, water, honey). The oils transfer to the solvent. The simplest pre-industrial method. The one that works for almost everything, given time.

These are the three doors. Behind them is the same room: a small bottle of liquid that smells like the plant, contains the plant's vocabulary, and can be used for medicine, food, fragrance, or simply the pleasure of having extracted something useful from a living thing with your own hands.

## The math, before the methods

I want to give you the math first because the methods only make sense once the math is in your head.

### Yield (the "how much oil is in the plant")

The essential oil content of a plant, by weight, varies from 0.01% (rose petals) to 7% (clove buds, some eucalyptus species). Most aromatic herbs are between 0.5% and 3%. A typical garden lavender, harvested at the right time and properly dried, is 1.5% oil by weight.

So: **100 g of dried lavender contains approximately 1.5 mL of essential oil.** That is the standard industrial yield and the standard home yield. The two converge because both are limited by the plant, not the equipment.

### Energy (the "how much heat does it take")

Steam distillation: to vaporize 1 L of water requires 2,260 kJ (the latent heat of vaporization at 100°C, 1 atm). To heat that water from 20°C to 100°C requires another 334 kJ (specific heat of water = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K)). The plant material in the still adds about 10% to the energy load.

To produce 5 mL of lavender oil from 450 g of fresh plant material (using 1.5 L of water in a 2 L still):

| Quantity | Value | Units |
|---|---|---|
| Plant material | 450 | g |
| Water in plant | ~315 | g (70% of fresh weight) |
| Added water | 1500 | g |
| Total water to heat and vaporize | 1815 | g |
| Energy to heat 20→100°C | 0.606 | MJ |
| Energy to vaporize | 4.102 | MJ |
| Total energy | 4.71 | MJ |
| In kWh | 1.31 | kWh |
| At $0.15/kWh | $0.20 | USD |
| At $0.30/kWh | $0.39 | USD |
| Output: lavender oil | 4.5 | mL (≈ 4 g) |
| Energy per mL of oil | 1.05 | MJ/mL |
| Cost per mL of oil (energy) | $0.04 | USD/mL |

The energy cost is trivial. The dominant cost is the plant material, the equipment, and your time.

### Recovery (the "how much of the oil actually comes out")

A perfect steam distillation recovers 100% of the oil. A real steam distillation recovers 60-90% of the oil. The losses are:
- Oil that stays in the plant material (5-15%)
- Oil that stays in the water (the hydrosol still has some oil in it; 2-10%)
- Oil lost to leaks, spills, incomplete condensation (1-5%)
- Oil that degrades from excessive heat (1-3%)

A well-built home still with cold condenser and proper sealing recovers 80-90% of the oil. A pressure cooker modification with copper coil recovers 70-80%. A kitchen pot with inverted lid recovers 50-70%. Solar distillation (passive, low temperature) recovers 60-80% over 4-8 hours of sun.

For a single 5 mL batch: expect 3-4.5 mL out of a kitchen-pot still. Expect 4-4.5 mL out of a pressure-cooker still. Expect 3-4 mL out of a solar still.

## The first principle, then the methods

In the framework's language, the first principle is the **substrate compressibility** of the cell wall.

The plant's cell wall is a synchronized network of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and pectin. The cell wall is the substrate's response to the plant's need for a rigid structure. The cell wall's compressibility is high in the radial direction (the cell can expand) and low in the longitudinal direction (the cell holds its shape). The cell wall is anisotropic.

The essential oil molecules — typically C10-C15 terpenes with molecular weights 130-220 g/mol — are too large to diffuse through the intact cell wall. The molecules are stored in dedicated glands. The glands have thinner walls and the molecules pass through them more easily.

The pre-formation principle (T2.8) applies: the oil must be **free** in the cell before it can be released. The pre-formation here is the disruption of the cell wall or the gland wall. The disruption is the rate-limiting step.

Three ways to disrupt:

1. **Mechanical disruption** (cutting, grinding, pressing). Breaks the glands open. Oil flows out. Fast, cheap, no heat. The pre-formation is the cut.

2. **Thermal disruption** (steam, hot water). Softens the cell wall and the gland wall. The gland wall's compressibility drops at ~50-70°C. The oil releases into the surrounding water or steam. The pre-formation is the heat. Standard steam distillation operates at 100°C, well above the disruption temperature.

3. **Solvent disruption** (oil, ethanol, vinegar, water). The solvent penetrates the cell wall by diffusion. The solvent dissolves the oil and carries it out. The pre-formation is the diffusion. Slow but cold.

The first principle is: **the cell wall is the bottleneck. The cell wall is the pre-formation. The cell wall is what you are paying to disrupt.**

This is why:
- **Cutting or bruising the plant before extraction doubles the yield** (T2.8 — the pre-formation principle). The cut is the disruption. The cut is the cheapest disruption.
- **Drying the plant before steam distillation often increases the yield** (the cell wall is more brittle, breaks more easily under steam). The dry cell wall is more pre-formed for disruption.
- **Adding salt to the water in steam distillation increases the yield slightly** (the salt water has a higher boiling point, more thermal energy per unit time, faster disruption).
- **A 30-minute soak in warm water before distillation increases the yield** (the soak is the pre-formation. The 30 minutes is the pre-formation time).
- **Chewing aromatic herbs releases the oil faster than grinding** (the teeth break the cell wall mechanically, the saliva is the solvent, the heat of the mouth is the thermal disruption — all three at once).

The framework says: the pre-formation is the test. The pre-formation is the time. The pre-formation is most of the work.

## The seven methods, ranked by cost and accessibility

I will give you the seven methods in the order from cheapest to most expensive, and in the order from most universal to most specialized.

### Method 1: Chewing and spitting (the original method)

**Cost**: $0.
**Time**: 10-30 minutes.
**Yield**: Variable, 10-50% of available oil.
**Equipment**: Your teeth.

This is the original method. The original method is the framework's first prediction: the substrate is the medium, the teeth are the disruption, the saliva is the solvent, the mouth is the warm container, the tongue is the separator. Chewing aromatic herbs releases the essential oils into the saliva. The saliva is mostly water (the substrate's natural solvent for small molecules). The oils are partially miscible in saliva. The mouth's heat (~37°C) is enough to mobilize the most volatile compounds.

The method is not as efficient as steam distillation. The method is free. The method is what every human has done with mint, basil, eucalyptus, and tobacco for at least 100,000 years. The framework says: this is the substrate. The substrate does not require apparatus. The substrate is the apparatus.

**Use case**: Sampling aromatic herbs in the field. Testing for quality. The original method is the framework's ground truth.

### Method 2: Cold pressing (citrus and seeds)

**Cost**: $0-5.
**Time**: 5-30 minutes.
**Yield**: 70-95% for citrus peels.
**Equipment**: Your hands, or a manual juicer, or a garlic press, or a stone.

Citrus peels are unique: the oil glands are at the surface, just under the cuticle. A modest mechanical force ruptures them. The oil sprays out. This is the basis of commercial cold-pressed citrus oil production: the peel is pressed against a slowly rotating abrasive surface, the glands rupture, the oil is washed off with water, the water-oil mixture is centrifuged to separate.

You can do this at home. Take a lemon peel. Hold it near a candle flame (carefully, with the peel a few inches away, peel side toward the flame). The heat expands the glands, the oil vaporizes, the vapor ignites — you see a brief flash of light. This is the "peel flash" demonstration. The flash is the oil igniting. The flash is the substrate releasing its volatile synchronizations.

Or: take a lemon peel, bend it sharply with the colored side out, watch the oil spray. The oil is visible as a fine mist. The oil is the substrate's stored language. The mist is the substrate speaking.

**Use case**: Citrus oils. The only method that works at room temperature. The cheapest method for the highest-volume oils (orange, lemon, grapefruit, bergamot, lime).

### Method 3: Solar distillation (the zero-electricity still)

**Cost**: $0-10.
**Time**: 4-8 hours per batch (sunny day).
**Yield**: 60-80% (validated against controlled distillation in published trials).
**Equipment**: A glass jar, a smaller glass jar or bowl, a rock or brick, plastic wrap or glass lid, a stone weight, and a sunny day.

The simplest possible still. A pit is dug, a smaller container placed in the center to catch the distillate, plant material is placed in the pit around the smaller container (or in a separate container on a brick above), the pit is covered with a clear plastic or glass sheet, a small stone is placed on the cover to create a low point where condensate drips into the collection container. The sun heats the air inside the pit. The air heats the plant. The plant's volatile oils vaporize along with water vapor. The vapor condenses on the clear cover. The condensate runs down to the low point. The condensate drips into the collection container.

This is the technology of the survival still, the desert still, the Mars-rover water-recovery still. It works for aromatic plants. A 2021 study in *Thermal Science* showed that solar distillation of eucalyptus produced oil of equivalent quantity and quality to controlled steam distillation, with essentially zero energy cost.

The framework's reading: the sun is the heat source. The substrate (sunlight) provides the energy to disrupt the cell walls and vaporize the oils. The plastic or glass cover is the condenser. The collection container is the separator. The substrate organizes all four.

**Use case**: Off-grid distillation, hot climates, educational demonstrations, the absolute first step. The framework's most economical still.

### Method 4: Kitchen-pot hydrodistillation (the $5 still)

**Cost**: $5-20.
**Time**: 1-3 hours per batch.
**Yield**: 50-70%.
**Equipment**: A large pot with a lid, a brick or steamer rack, water, plant material, a collection bowl.

The simplest practical still. The pot is filled with water to about 1/3 capacity. The plant material is placed on a brick or steamer rack above the water (to prevent burning). The lid is inverted (concave side down) so that condensate collects at the low center. Ice or cold water is placed on top of the inverted lid to improve condensation. The pot is heated. The water boils. The steam rises through the plant material. The steam carries the volatile oils. The vapor hits the cold lid. The vapor condenses. The condensate runs to the center. The condensate drips off the lid into a collection bowl placed below the lid's center.

Variations:
- **With a hole in the lid and a tube**: drill a 1/2-inch hole in the lid, fit a copper or aluminum tube through it (or just bend a piece of sheet metal into a channel), direct the condensate to an external collection vessel. This is the classic "moonshine still" geometry. Yield improves to 60-80%.
- **With a brick or stone weight on the lid**: improves the seal and reduces leaks.
- **With salt in the water (1-2% by weight)**: raises the boiling point slightly, more thermal energy per unit time, slightly better yield.

**Use case**: Small batches at home. The first practical step beyond solar. The framework's most common still.

### Method 5: Pressure-cooker still (the $20 still)

**Cost**: $15-50.
**Time**: 1-2 hours per batch.
**Yield**: 70-85%.
**Equipment**: A pressure cooker (4-8 quart), copper tubing (3 m of 10 mm, ~$7-15), a bucket or tub for the cold-water bath, a collection jar, fittings.

The pressure cooker is the home distiller's best friend. The pressure cooker is rated for 1-1.5 atm of internal pressure. The pressure raises the boiling point to ~115-125°C. The higher temperature means faster extraction. The pressure means the oil-water vapor mixture is pushed through the tubing more efficiently. The seal is built-in.

The build: drill or punch a hole in the pressure cooker lid (1/2 to 1 inch diameter, near the edge). Fit a copper compression coupling through the hole, sealed with food-grade silicone and a copper washer. Attach 3-10 m of 10 mm copper tubing to the coupling. Coil the tubing and submerge it in a bucket of cold water (or flowing water). The other end of the tubing drips into a collection jar.

The pressure cooker has the safety valve. The safety valve is important. Do not block it. The safety valve is what prevents the still from becoming a bomb.

This is the design that has been documented in dozens of Instructables and YouTube tutorials. The framework's reading: the pressure cooker is the substrate's enclosed chamber. The copper tubing is the substrate's phase-transition channel. The cold water bath is the substrate's condenser. The collection jar is the substrate's separator. The whole assembly is the substrate's distillation apparatus, built from kitchen materials.

**Use case**: The home distiller's workhorse. The first step toward serious small-scale production. The framework's most versatile still.

### Method 6: Solvent extraction (the $5 chemistry)

**Cost**: $0-15.
**Time**: 2-6 weeks (maceration), 1-3 hours (reflux).
**Yield**: 50-90% depending on solvent.
**Equipment**: Mason jar, solvent (water, vinegar, ethanol, oil, honey), plant material, optionally a pot for reflux.

For compounds that are not volatile (alkaloids, glycosides, flavonoids, tannins, fixed oils, resins, waxes), distillation doesn't work. You need a solvent.

The general principle: **"like dissolves like"**. Polar solvents (water, vinegar, ethanol) extract polar compounds (alkaloids, glycosides, tannins, flavonoids). Non-polar solvents (oils, hexane) extract non-polar compounds (fixed oils, resins, waxes). Intermediate solvents (45-90% ethanol) extract a broad range.

Specific solvents, ranked by cost and accessibility:

| Solvent | Cost | Best for | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water (decoction)** | $0 | Tannins, some glycosides, water-soluble alkaloids | Boil 15-60 min. Drink within 24h. |
| **Apple cider vinegar (5%)** | $0.50-3/L | Alkaloids, minerals, some flavonoids | 2-4 weeks maceration, shake daily. |

**The hierarchy, by intent**:
- Want a calming evening drink? Decoction (water + herb, 20 min simmer).
- Want a long-lasting medicine? Vinegar tincture (2-4 weeks).
- Want a potent broad-spectrum extract? Ethanol tincture (4-6 weeks).
- Want a topical oil for skin or muscle? Oil infusion (2-4 weeks).
- Want a sweet medicinal syrup? Honey extraction (2-4 weeks).
- Want to extract a non-polar resin or fixed oil that won't come out with water? Higher-proof ethanol or food-grade oil (longer, with heat).

**Use case**: Everything that distillation can't do. The framework's most versatile method. The method that does not require a still at all.

### Method 7: Enfleurage (the original perfume method)

**Cost**: $0-10.
**Time**: 1-3 days (cold) or 1-2 weeks (warm).
**Yield**: 70-95% for delicate flowers.
**Equipment**: Animal fat (lard, tallow) or a solid vegetable fat (coconut, shea), a flat tray, plant material (delicate flowers like jasmine, tuberose, rose, gardenia).

The original perfume method. Used for thousands of years. The fat is spread thinly on a glass plate. Fresh flowers are laid on the fat. The fat absorbs the volatile oils. The flowers are replaced daily. The fat becomes saturated with the flower oil. The fat is called a "pomade". The pomade is washed with alcohol to extract the absolute.

You can do this with tallow (rendered beef or mutton fat), lard, or coconut oil. The cold version takes 1-3 days per flower change. The warm version (slightly above room temperature) takes 1-2 weeks. The yield is high. The product is the original perfume.

**Use case**: Delicate flowers whose oils are destroyed by heat. The original method. The framework's most patient method.

The seven methods require equipment. The five devices below are the cheapest possible equipment for each method. Total cost: **$0-15 across all five.**

### Device 1: The $0 Solar Pit Still

**Materials**: A shovel, a sheet of clear plastic or glass (1 m × 1 m), a small stone, a glass jar or bowl (250-500 mL), a black-painted jar or tin can (to absorb heat).

**Build**: Dig a pit 30-50 cm deep. Place the black-painted jar in the center of the pit (this is the heat absorber). Place the collection bowl next to the black jar. Pile plant material around the jars. Cover the pit with the clear plastic or glass, with a small stone in the center to create a low point above the collection bowl. Seal the edges of the plastic with soil. Wait 4-8 hours of sun.

**Yield**: 60-80% of standard steam distillation. **Cost**: $0-2. **Skill**: 5 minutes of digging.

### Device 2: The $5 Kitchen-Pot Still

**Materials**: A large pot with a lid (any metal pot, ≥ 4 L), a brick or steamer rack, a smaller bowl that fits inside the pot, water, plant material.

**Build**: Place the brick in the bottom of the pot. Add water to just below the top of the brick. Place the plant material on the brick. Invert the lid (concave side down) and place it on the pot. Heat on a stove at medium. Place ice on top of the inverted lid to improve condensation. The condensate runs to the center of the lid and drips into the smaller bowl placed below the center.

**Yield**: 50-70%. **Cost**: $5. **Skill**: 5 minutes of assembly.

### Device 3: The $7 Pressure-Cooker Still

**Materials**: A pressure cooker (4-8 quart, ~$5-25 used or new), 3 m of 10 mm copper tubing (~$7-15), a compression coupling to fit the pressure cooker lid, food-grade silicone, a bucket or tub for the cold bath, a collection jar.

**Build**: Drill or punch a 1/2-inch hole in the pressure cooker lid. Fit the compression coupling through the hole, sealed with silicone and a copper washer. Attach the copper tubing to the coupling. Coil the tubing and submerge it in the cold water bath. The other end drips into a collection jar. Place plant material and water in the pressure cooker. Heat on a stove. The pressure and temperature rise. The vapor is forced through the tubing. The vapor condenses in the cold bath. The condensate drips into the jar.

**Yield**: 70-85%. **Cost**: $7-15 (just the tubing if you have a pressure cooker). **Skill**: 30 minutes, basic tools.

### Device 4: The $0 Mason Jar Tincture

**Materials**: A mason jar with a lid, plant material, any solvent (water, vinegar, vodka, oil, honey).

**Build**: Fill the jar 1/3 to 1/2 full of plant material. Cover with solvent. Seal the lid. Shake. Wait 2-6 weeks, shaking daily. Strain through cheesecloth. Bottle. Done.

**Yield**: 50-90% depending on solvent and time. **Cost**: $0-3. **Skill**: 2 minutes.

### Device 5: The $0 Chewing Method (no device required)

**Materials**: Your teeth, your saliva, your time.

**Build**: Chew aromatic herbs. Swallow the water-soluble fraction. Spit out the fiber. The oil is in your mouth and partially absorbed through the oral mucosa.

**Yield**: 10-50% depending on herb and duration. **Cost**: $0. **Skill**: 0 minutes.
