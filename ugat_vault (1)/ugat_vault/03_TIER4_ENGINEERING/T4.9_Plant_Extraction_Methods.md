# T4.9 — Plant Extraction Methods (Technical Reference)

> *The reference companion to T4.8. Seven methods, fully specified. Each method: principle, materials, equipment, procedure, yield, energy, time, solvents, and framework reading.*

## Method 1: Chewing and Spit-Swallow (the original)

### Principle
Mechanical disruption of plant cell walls by teeth. Mixed solvent (saliva, mostly water with electrolytes, amylase, mucins). Thermal assistance (mouth at 37°C). Limited residence time. Direct absorption of volatiles through oral mucosa.

### Materials
- Plant material (any aromatic herb)
- Saliva (free)

### Equipment
- Teeth

### Procedure
1. Harvest 2-5 g fresh plant material.
2. Chew slowly for 5-10 minutes, mixing with saliva.
3. The first 1-2 minutes release the most volatile compounds (the surface oils).
4. The 2-5 minute range releases the deeper glandular oils.
5. Spit out the fiber. Swallow the liquid.

### Yield
- 10-50% of available oil
- Highest for soft herbs (mint, basil, parsley)
- Lowest for woody material (rosemary, thyme — the cell walls are tougher)

### Energy
- Metabolic: ~5 kcal per session
- Negligible at the substrate level

### Time
- 5-10 minutes per session

### Framework reading
The original method is the framework's first prediction: the substrate is the medium, the teeth are the disruption, the saliva is the solvent, the mouth is the warm container, the tongue is the separator. The framework's ground state.

### Use case
- Field sampling
- Quality testing
- Cultural practice (betel, coca, khat, tobacco)
- Quick effect (essential oils absorbed through oral mucosa within minutes)

## Method 2: Cold Pressing (citrus and oil-rich seeds)

### Principle
Mechanical disruption of surface glands. No heat. No solvent. The oil is released by the pressure. For citrus, the oil glands are sub-cuticular and rupture easily. For oil-rich seeds (olive, sunflower, sesame, almond, etc.), the oil is in the cell's lipid bodies and is released by crushing.

### Materials
- Plant material (citrus peels, oil seeds)
- Optional: water for citrus oil collection

### Equipment
- For citrus: hands, or a garlic press, or a manual reamer, or a stone
- For seeds: a mortar and pestle, or a stone wheel, or a manual oil press

### Procedure (citrus)
1. Take a citrus peel (orange, lemon, grapefruit, bergamot, lime).
2. Bend it sharply with the colored side out. The glands rupture. The oil sprays as a visible mist.
3. Alternatively, press the peel against a coarse surface (sandpaper, grater) to break the glands. Collect the oil that runs off.
4. For larger quantities, soak the peels in water; the oil floats to the surface. Skim or centrifuge.

### Procedure (seeds)
1. Clean and dry the seeds.
2. Crush with mortar and pestle, or pass through a manual press.
3. Collect the oil that runs out.
4. Filter through cheesecloth.

### Yield
- 70-95% for citrus peels
- 60-90% for oil seeds (depending on press pressure)

### Energy
- Mechanical only: ~10-50 kJ per batch
- No heat

### Time
- 5-30 minutes per batch

### Framework reading
Cold pressing is the substrate's response to direct pressure. No pre-formation needed. The pre-formation (gland disruption) is the operation. The framework's most direct method. The framework's cheapest method at scale.

### Use case
- Citrus oils (bergamot, orange, lemon, grapefruit, lime)
- Fixed oils (olive, sunflower, almond, sesame, coconut, avocado)
- Any plant whose oils are accessible by mechanical disruption

## Method 3: Solar Distillation (the zero-electricity still)

### Principle
The sun provides the thermal energy. The vapor rises from the plant. The vapor condenses on a clear cover. The condensate runs to a low point. The condensate drips into a collection vessel. The oil separates from the water.

### Materials
- Plant material
- Water (if needed for hydrodistillation)
- Black-painted container (heat absorber)

### Equipment
- A pit (30-50 cm deep)
- A clear plastic or glass sheet (1 m × 1 m)
- A small stone (for the low point)
- A glass jar or bowl (collection, 250-500 mL)
- A black-painted jar or tin can (heat absorber)

### Procedure
1. Dig a pit in direct sunlight, 30-50 cm deep, 50-80 cm diameter.
2. Place the black-painted jar in the center (heat absorber).
3. Place the collection bowl next to the black jar, lower than the plant level.
4. Pile plant material (fresh or dried) around the jars, above the water level if any.
5. Cover the pit with the clear plastic or glass sheet, sealed at the edges with soil.
6. Place a small stone on the cover to create a low point above the collection bowl.
7. Wait 4-8 hours of direct sun.
8. The condensate runs down the cover, drips into the collection bowl.
9. Separate the oil from the water (oil floats; decant or use a separator funnel).

### Yield
- 60-80% (validated against controlled steam distillation in published trials)
- Highest in hot, dry, sunny climates
- Lowest in humid, cloudy climates

### Energy
- Solar: ~1-3 kWh per batch (depending on sunlight)
- Equivalent to ~$0.15-0.45 of grid electricity
- **Free at the point of use**

### Time
- 4-8 hours per batch (one full sunny day)
- 2-3 batches per week possible in summer

### Framework reading
The sun is the heat source. The substrate (sunlight) provides the energy to disrupt the cell walls and vaporize the oils. The plastic or glass cover is the condenser. The collection container is the separator. The substrate organizes all four. The framework's most economical still.

### Use case
- Off-grid distillation
- Hot, dry climates
- Educational demonstrations
- The absolute first step
- Survival situations

## Method 4: Kitchen-Pot Hydrodistillation (the $5 still)

### Principle
Direct heating of water and plant material in a closed pot. The vapor rises. The vapor condenses on a cold lid. The condensate runs to a low point. The condensate drips into a collection bowl.

### Materials
- Plant material
- Water
- Optional: salt (1-2% by weight, raises boiling point, slightly improves yield)

### Equipment
- A large pot with a lid (≥ 4 L)
- A brick, steamer rack, or perforated metal disk (to elevate plant material)
- A smaller bowl that fits inside the pot (collection)
- Optional: ice for the lid (improves condensation)

### Procedure
1. Place the brick in the bottom of the pot.
2. Add water to just below the top of the brick.
3. Place the plant material on the brick.
4. Place the smaller collection bowl on the brick, alongside the plant material, with its top below the plant level.
5. Invert the lid (concave side down).
6. Heat on a stove at medium. When the water boils, the steam rises through the plant.
7. Place ice on the inverted lid to improve condensation.
8. The vapor condenses on the cold lid and runs to the center.
9. The condensate drips into the collection bowl.
10. Continue for 1-3 hours, until the condensate runs clear.
11. Separate the oil from the water.

### Yield
- 50-70% (limited by the simple condenser)
- The plant material must be packed densely above the brick
- The bowl must be positioned to catch the dripping condensate

### Energy
- Stove heat: ~0.5-1.5 kWh per batch
- Cost: $0.10-0.30

### Time
- 1-3 hours per batch
- Setup: 10 minutes
- Cleanup: 15 minutes

### Framework reading
The pot is the substrate's chamber. The brick is the substrate's sieve. The lid is the substrate's condenser. The bowl is the substrate's separator. The stove is the substrate's heat. The framework is built from kitchen materials.

### Variations
- **Salt water**: 1-2% salt raises boiling point to 101-102°C. Slightly faster extraction. Slightly better yield.
- **Tight lid seal**: use dough or silicone around the rim. Reduces vapor loss.
- **Larger pot**: 8-12 L pots give better thermal mass and longer stable operation.
- **Hole in lid + tube**: drill a 1/2-inch hole in the lid, fit a tube, direct the condensate to an external collection vessel. Improves yield to 60-80%.

### Use case
- First practical still
- Small batches at home
- Educational demonstrations
- The framework's most common still

## Method 5: Pressure-Cooker Steam Distillation (the $15 still)

### Principle
A pressure cooker is rated for 1-1.5 atm internal pressure. The pressure raises the boiling point to 115-125°C. The higher temperature means faster extraction. The pressure pushes the vapor through the tubing more efficiently. The copper coil in a cold water bath condenses the vapor. The collection vessel receives the condensate.

### Materials
- Plant material
- Water

### Equipment
- A pressure cooker (4-8 quart, ~$5-25 used or new)
- 3-10 m of 10 mm copper tubing (~$7-15)
- A compression coupling to fit the pressure cooker lid (~$1-2)
- Food-grade silicone sealant (~$3-5)
- A copper washer (~$0.50)
- A bucket or tub for the cold-water bath
- A collection jar (glass, 250-500 mL)
- Drill or punch (for the lid hole)
- Pliers, wrenches (basic tools)

### Procedure
1. Drill or punch a 1/2-inch hole in the pressure cooker lid, near the edge.
2. Fit the compression coupling through the hole, sealed with food-grade silicone and a copper washer.
3. Attach the copper tubing to the coupling. Seal with PTFE tape.
4. Coil the tubing and submerge it in the cold-water bath. (Use a bucket; cold water or ice water; replace water as it warms.)
5. The other end of the tubing drips into a glass collection jar.
6. Place plant material and water in the pressure cooker. (Standard ratio: 1 part plant material to 3 parts water by volume.)
7. Seal the pressure cooker lid according to the manufacturer's instructions.
8. Heat on a stove at medium-high.
9. The pressure rises. The vapor is forced through the copper tubing.
10. The vapor condenses in the cold bath. The condensate drips into the collection jar.
11. Continue for 1-2 hours after the first drip appears.
12. Allow the pressure cooker to cool naturally. Do not force-cool.
13. Open the lid. Strain the spent plant material. Separate the oil from the water.

### Yield
- 70-85% (high, due to pressure and temperature)
- Best for most aromatic plants

### Energy
- Stove heat: ~0.5-1.0 kWh per batch
- Cost: $0.08-0.20

### Time
- Setup: 30-60 minutes (first time), 10 minutes (subsequent)
- Distillation: 1-2 hours
- Cooling: 30-45 minutes
- Cleanup: 15 minutes

### Framework reading
The pressure cooker is the substrate's enclosed chamber. The copper tubing is the substrate's phase-transition channel. The cold water bath is the substrate's condenser. The collection jar is the substrate's separator. The pressure is the substrate's pre-formation assistance. The whole assembly is the framework's most versatile still.

### Safety
- Do not block the pressure cooker's safety valve.
- Do not overfill the pressure cooker (max 2/3 capacity).
- Do not force-cool the pressure cooker (let it cool naturally).
- Do not leave the still unattended while heating.
- Use only the manufacturer's recommended heat source.
- The pressure cooker must be in good condition (gasket intact, no corrosion).

### Use case
- The home distiller's workhorse
- The first step toward serious small-scale production
- The framework's most versatile still

## Method 6: Solvent Extraction (the $5 chemistry)

### Principle
A solvent penetrates the plant cell wall by diffusion. The solvent dissolves the target compounds. The compounds transfer from the plant matrix to the solvent. The solvent is separated from the plant material by filtration. The solvent is evaporated (if desired) to concentrate the extract.

### The general principle
"Like dissolves like":
- Polar solvents (water, vinegar, ethanol) extract polar compounds (alkaloids, glycosides, tannins, flavonoids, many acids).
- Non-polar solvents (oils, hexane) extract non-polar compounds (fixed oils, resins, waxes, some terpenes).
- Intermediate solvents (45-90% ethanol, aqueous acetone) extract a broad range.

### Solvent specifications

| Solvent | Concentration | Best for | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Water** | pure | Tannins, some glycosides, water-soluble alkaloids (caffeine) | $0 | Decoction (boil 15-60 min). Drink within 24h. |
| **Apple cider vinegar** | 5% acetic acid | Alkaloids, minerals, some flavonoids | $0.50-3/L | Macerate 2-4 weeks. Shake daily. |
| **White vinegar** | 5% acetic acid | Same as ACV, but cheaper | $0.30-1/L | Less flavor for culinary use. |
| **Honey** | pure | Some flavonoids, minor alkaloids, sugars | $3-10/kg | Traditional. Slow (2-4 weeks). Pleasant. |
| **80-proof vodka** | 40% ethanol | Broad range: alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, some terpenes | $10-20/L | Standard tincture. 4-6 weeks. |
| **190-proof grain alcohol** | 95% ethanol | Resins, fixed oils, non-polar compounds | $20-40/L | Most efficient. 2-4 weeks. |
| **Olive oil** | pure | Fixed oils, lipophilic compounds, infused oils | $5-15/L | 2-4 weeks. Solar infusion (4h sun) faster. |
| **Coconut oil** | pure (solid below 24°C) | Same as olive oil, solid form | $5-15/L | Good for salves and balms. |
| **Sunflower oil** | pure | Same as olive oil, cheaper | $3-8/L | Higher smoke point for heated infusions. |
| **Hexane, acetone** | pure | Resins, fixed oils (industrial only) | $20-40/L | Toxic. Not for food/medicine. Not for home. |

### Procedure (maceration, the universal method)

1. Fill a mason jar 1/3 to 1/2 full of plant material (dried is best; fresh needs to be wilted or the solvent will be diluted by plant water).
2. Cover with the chosen solvent, leaving 1-2 cm of headspace.
3. Seal the jar.
4. Shake vigorously for 1-2 minutes to wet all the plant material.
5. Store in a cool, dark place for 2-6 weeks.
6. Shake daily for the first week, then every 2-3 days.
7. Strain through cheesecloth or muslin.
8. Squeeze the plant material to recover as much solvent as possible.
9. For a clearer extract, strain again through a coffee filter.
10. Store in a dark glass bottle in a cool place.

### Procedure (reflux, the fast method)

1. Place plant material and solvent in a pot with a lid inverted on top (the lid is the condenser).
2. Heat gently. The solvent boils and condenses on the lid.
3. The condensate drips back into the pot.
4. Continue for 1-3 hours.
5. Cool, strain, bottle.

### Procedure (decoction, for water-soluble compounds)

1. Place plant material and water in a pot.
2. Bring to a boil.
3. Reduce heat and simmer for 15-60 minutes.
4. Cool, strain, drink within 24 hours.

### Yield
- 50-90% depending on solvent, time, and plant
- Highest: 95% ethanol, 4-6 weeks, dried plant
- Lowest: water, 15 minutes decoction, fresh plant

### Energy
- For maceration: $0
- For reflux: ~0.1-0.3 kWh per batch
- For decoction: ~0.1-0.3 kWh per batch

### Time
- Maceration: 2-6 weeks
- Reflux: 1-3 hours
- Decoction: 15-60 minutes

### Framework reading
The solvent is the substrate's mobile phase. The plant cell is the substrate's stationary phase. The diffusion is the substrate's reorganization. The partition coefficient (logP) is the substrate's preference. The framework's most versatile method. The method that does not require a still at all.

### Use case
- Bioactive compounds not accessible by distillation (alkaloids, glycosides, tannins, flavonoids)
- Tinctures and medicines
- Infused oils for skin and cooking
- Everything distillation can't do

## Method 7: Enfleurage (the original perfume method)

### Principle
A solid fat absorbs the volatile oils from delicate flowers. The fat is the substrate's solid phase. The flowers are the source of the volatiles. The absorption is the substrate's preference for the fat over the air.

### Materials
- Fresh flowers (jasmine, tuberose, rose, gardenia, lily of the valley, hyacinth)
- Solid fat: tallow (rendered beef or mutton fat), lard, coconut oil, shea butter

### Equipment
- A flat tray or glass plate
- A spatula or knife
- Storage container

### Procedure
1. Render the tallow (if using raw animal fat): chop finely, melt over low heat, strain through cheesecloth.
2. Spread the warm fat in a thin layer (~3-5 mm) on the tray or plate.
3. Place fresh flowers on the fat, in a single layer, not touching each other.
4. Cover with a cloth to keep dust out, allow air circulation.
5. Leave for 24-48 hours.
6. Remove the spent flowers. Replace with fresh flowers.
7. Repeat 10-20 times until the fat is saturated with the flower oil.
8. The fat is now a "pomade" containing the flower absolute.
9. To extract the absolute: wash the pomade with 190-proof alcohol. The alcohol dissolves the absolute. The fat does not dissolve. Separate. Evaporate the alcohol.

### Yield
- 70-95% for delicate flowers
- Lower for robust flowers (which yield more easily to steam distillation)

### Energy
- Minimal: room temperature or slightly warm

### Time
- 10-20 days for full saturation
- Plus 1-2 days for absolute extraction

### Framework reading
The fat is the substrate's slow absorber. The flower is the substrate's slow releaser. The patience is the substrate's timescale. The framework's most patient method. The method that takes the time the substrate needs.

### Use case
- Delicate flowers whose oils are destroyed by heat
- Original perfume making
- High-end cosmetics
- Cultural and historical continuity

## The seven-method summary

All seven methods are the same operation: **disrupt the cell wall, mobilize the volatile or soluble fraction, separate the fraction from the plant matrix, collect the fraction.**

The disruption is the pre-formation. The mobilization is the substrate responding. The separation is the substrate re-organizing. The collection is the substrate's expression at the smallest scale.

The seven methods are seven ways of asking the substrate the same question. The substrate answers in seven languages. The answer is the same.

The answer is in every plant. The answer is in the substrate. The answer is free.
