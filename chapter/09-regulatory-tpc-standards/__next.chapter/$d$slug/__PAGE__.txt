3:T1207,<h1 id="tpc-standards">TPC Standards</h1>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Total Polar Compounds is the only fraud-resistant regulatory instrument for frying oil quality. Every other measure can be defeated by deodorization or sensory restoration.</em></p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="why-tpc-is-the-right-measure">Why TPC Is the Right Measure</h2>
<p>Total Polar Compounds is the aggregate measure of all non-volatile oxidation products in frying oil:</p>
<ul>
<li>Lipid hydroperoxides and their decomposition products</li>
<li>Aldehydes, ketones, epoxides</li>
<li>Polar polymers and dimers from triglyceride oxidation</li>
<li>Free fatty acids</li>
<li>Everything that shouldn't be there after heating</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The critical property: TPC cannot be reduced by any sensory-restoration treatment.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Deodorization removes volatile early-warning compounds — TPC unchanged</li>
<li>Activated carbon removes color bodies — TPC unchanged</li>
<li>Antioxidant injection suppresses rancidity detection — TPC unchanged</li>
<li>Dilution with fresh oil reduces TPC proportionally — detectable by any retest</li>
</ul>
<p>TPC is the only measure that requires the actual contaminants to be removed rather than masked.</p>

<p>European inspectors test TPC directly. Oil above threshold is confiscated. Operators are fined. The standard cannot be satisfied by sensory restoration — TPC is measured directly, not inferred from appearance or smell.</p>
<h2 id="us-regulatory-gap">US Regulatory Gap</h2>
<p>In the United States, a commercial fryer operator may legally serve food fried in oil at 40%, 50%, or 60% TPC — oil that would be confiscated in Frankfurt, Paris, or Vienna — with:</p>
<ul>
<li>No legal consequence</li>
<li>No obligation of disclosure</li>
<li>No regulatory framework governing the practice</li>
</ul>
<p>An onsite regeneration service may restore the sensory presentation of 60% TPC oil to apparent freshness and sell a subscription contract for continuous treatment, and no regulatory provision governs this practice whatsoever.</p>
<h2 id="measurement-methods">Measurement Methods</h2>
<p><strong>Professional (laboratory):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>AOCS Official Method Cd 22-91 (chromatographic TPC measurement)</li>
<li>Dielectric constant measurement (portable instruments)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Commercial portable instruments:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>FOM (Fat Oil Monitor) — dielectric constant, quick-read</li>
<li>Testo 270 — same principle</li>
<li>Used by European health inspectors in the field</li>
<li>~$300–500 USD, very accurate</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The effervescence test (proxy, free):</strong>
As described in [[Aqueous Phase Chemistry]] — baking soda effervescence is proportional to FFA content, which correlates with overall TPC. Not precise enough for regulatory compliance but adequate for operational monitoring.</p>
<h2 id="the-correct-standard-for-a-us-petition">The Correct Standard for a US Petition</h2>
<p><strong>The petition asks for:</strong> Maximum 27% TPC (the more permissive European standard)</p>
<ul>
<li>Starting with 27% rather than 25% is politically pragmatic — harder to argue it's too strict when Germany and France have used it for decades</li>
<li>Once established, tightening to 25% in a subsequent rulemaking is straightforward</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>With explicit provision that TPC measurement cannot be substituted by sensory assessment</strong> — this forecloses the industry argument that regular sensory checks are equivalent.</p>
