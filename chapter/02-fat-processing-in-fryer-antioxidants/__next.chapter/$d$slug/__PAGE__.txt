3:T1705,<h1 id="in-fryer-antioxidants">In-Fryer Antioxidants</h1>
<blockquote>
<p><em>Real-time radical chain termination during the fry cycle. Addresses the problem at the source rather than treating the product.</em></p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="the-mechanism">The Mechanism</h2>
<p>Lipid peroxidation is a chain reaction:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Initiation:</strong> metal catalyst or energy removes H• from a PUFA double bond</li>
<li><strong>Propagation:</strong> LOO• + LH → LOOH + L• (repeating, exponential)</li>
<li><strong>Termination:</strong> antioxidant donates H• to LOO•, forming stable radical that doesn't propagate</li>
</ol>
<p>Polyphenol antioxidants work at the propagation step — they interrupt the chain by donating hydrogen atoms to lipid peroxy radicals (LOO•) faster than those radicals can abstract hydrogen from another lipid molecule.</p>
<p>Metal chelation addresses initiation — by removing Fe²⁺ and Cu²⁺ from the fat phase, the Fenton reaction (Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → OH•) is prevented from initiating new chains.</p>
<h2 id="the-herb-blend">The Herb Blend</h2>
<h3 id="rosemary-rosmarinus-officinalis">Rosemary (Rosmarinus officinalis)</h3>
<p><strong>Active compounds:</strong> Carnosic acid, carnosol
<strong>logP:</strong> ~4–5 (highly lipophilic — strong fat phase partitioning)
<strong>Thermal stability:</strong> Stable to ~200°C — survives frying temperatures
<strong>Mechanism:</strong> Radical chain termination + tocopherol regeneration
<strong>Regulatory status:</strong> EU-approved food antioxidant (E392)
<strong>Note:</strong> Most effective single addition. If only one herb, this is it.</p>
<h3 id="green-tea-camellia-sinensis">Green Tea (Camellia sinensis)</h3>
<p><strong>Active compound:</strong> EGCG (epigallocatechin gallate)
<strong>logP:</strong> ~1.5 (moderately lipophilic — meaningful fat partitioning)
<strong>Mechanism:</strong> Iron chelation (removes initiation catalyst), radical termination, tocopherol regeneration
<strong>Note:</strong> Caffeine (logP −0.07) does NOT partition into fat. Only polyphenols transfer.</p>
<h3 id="olive-leaf-olea-europaea">Olive Leaf (Olea europaea)</h3>
<p><strong>Active compound:</strong> Oleuropein → hydroxytyrosol (in situ thermal conversion)
<strong>Conversion:</strong> Oleuropein thermally hydrolyzes at frying temperatures to hydroxytyrosol
<strong>EFSA health claim:</strong> Specifically for olive polyphenols protecting LDL from oxidative damage
<strong>Mechanism:</strong> EGCG-equivalent iron chelation + additional NF-κB inhibition from hydroxytyrosol
<strong>Note:</strong> The thermal conversion is beneficial — hydroxytyrosol is more bioavailable than intact oleuropein</p>
<h3 id="thyme-thymus-vulgaris">Thyme (Thymus vulgaris)</h3>
<p><strong>Active compound:</strong> Thymol
<strong>logP:</strong> ~3.3 (good fat partitioning)
<strong>Mechanism:</strong> Radical chain termination, phenolic antioxidant</p>
<h3 id="sage-salvia-officinalis">Sage (Salvia officinalis)</h3>
<p><strong>Active compound:</strong> Carnosol (same as rosemary)
<strong>Mechanism:</strong> Complementary to rosemary through same diterpene phenolic class
<strong>Note:</strong> Synergistic with rosemary rather than redundant — additive protection</p>
<h2 id="partition-coefficients-why-they-matter">Partition Coefficients — Why They Matter</h2>
<p>The fat-phase antioxidant function requires compounds that PARTITION INTO FAT during frying. logP (octanol-water partition coefficient) predicts this:</p>
<ul>
<li>logP > 1.5: meaningful fat phase partitioning → effective in fryer</li>
<li>logP &#x3C; 0: prefers water → does NOT transfer into fat</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="practical-protocol">Practical Protocol</h2>
<p>Add a small handful (3–5g each) at the START of each frying session:</p>
<ul>
<li>Dried rosemary</li>
<li>Dried green tea leaves (cheap loose leaf, not expensive tea)</li>
<li>Dried olive leaf (from herbal supplier)</li>
<li>Dried thyme or sage</li>
</ul>
<p>Herbs fry along with food — no separate step required. Flavor contribution to food is mild and generally pleasant. These are cooking herbs used this way across multiple culinary traditions.</p>
<h2 id="the-tea-leaves-paradox">The Tea Leaves Paradox</h2>
<p>Dried tea leaves can be eaten after frying (they're food). After extended frying they have:</p>
<ul>
<li>Transferred their fat-soluble polyphenols into the fat (good — they've done their job)</li>
<li>Accumulated some fat-phase oxidized compounds from the frying environment</li>
<li>Become concentrated in the fraction that the starch-matrix adsorption research shows</li>
</ul>
<p>The French "potato cleaning cycle" exploits this — starchy foods adsorb oxidized compounds from fat during frying. Tea leaves do the same during frying while also donating antioxidants. Both effects are real and both are useful.</p>
