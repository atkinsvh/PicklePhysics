# Regulatory Compliance Path

> *Selling as a traditional fermented food product without health claims. This is both the correct legal positioning and the honest one — the product is food.*

## The Regulatory Fork

### Path A — Dietary Supplement
- Registration: DSHEA (Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act, 1994)
- Claims: Structure-function claims permitted with specific disclaimer ("This statement has not been evaluated by the FDA...")
- Manufacturing: 21 CFR Part 111 (dietary supplement GMP)
- Expensive, burdensome, requires specific labeling regime
- Not appropriate — the product is a fermented food, not a concentrated extract

### Path B — Conventional Food (Correct Path)
- Regulation: 21 CFR Parts 100–199 (food standards)
- Claims: No structure-function or health claims without specific FDA authorization
- Manufacturing: 21 CFR Part 117 (food GMP for human food)
- The honest positioning: a fermented food product
- Significantly simpler compliance path

## Key Food Safety Regulations

### 21 CFR Part 117 — Current Good Manufacturing Practice, Hazard Analysis, and Risk-Based Preventive Controls for Human Food (FSMA)
- Requires: Written food safety plan, hazard analysis, preventive controls
- Applicable to: All food facilities with >$1M annual sales (Qualified Facility exemption below this threshold)
- For a small startup below $1M: Qualified Facility status with modified requirements
- Still requires: Basic sanitation, allergen controls, pest control, equipment maintenance

### pH and Water Activity Controls (LACF Regulations for Acidified Foods)
**This is the critical compliance issue for lactofermented products.**

The FDA distinguishes between:
- **Fermented foods** (naturally acidified through microbial activity): not regulated as "acidified foods"
- **Acidified foods** (acid added to reach target pH): 21 CFR Part 114 — requires process filing with FDA

A **genuine lactoferment** (where the pH drop is produced entirely by microbial lactic acid production, not added acid) is classified as a fermented food, not an acidified food. This is the correct classification for this product.

**Documentation required:**
- pH monitoring records showing gradual pH decrease consistent with fermentation (not an immediate drop from added acid)
- Evidence that the pH reduction is microbially produced
- Final pH ≤4.6 (which prevents Clostridium botulinum growth — the primary safety concern in low-acid food production)

Target pH of 3.2–3.8 is well below the 4.6 safety threshold.

### Allergen Labeling (21 CFR Part 101.4)
The formulation contains potential allergens:
- **Celery** (European allergen, not US top 9)
- **Wheat germ** — contains gluten; must be labeled as containing wheat (US top 9 allergen)
- Review full botanical list against FDA's top 9: milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybean, sesame

Sesame (added to top 9 in 2023) — check botanical pack for sesame content.

## Label Requirements

**Required on label:**
- Product name
- Net weight
- Ingredient list (all ingredients, in descending order by weight, including all botanicals)
- Manufacturer name and address
- Nutrition Facts panel (can use simplified format for certain products)
- Allergen declaration

**Prohibited without authorization:**
- Disease claims ("helps with MCAS")
- Unapproved structure-function claims tied to body parts or physiological conditions
- Anything implying the product treats, cures, prevents, or mitigates a disease

**Permitted:**
- "Traditional fermented preparation"
- "Small-batch, 120-day fermented"
- List of ingredients as botanicals
- "May contain histamine" advisory for MCAS-relevant customers (voluntary but valuable)

## State-Level Requirements

Some states have additional requirements for cottage food vs. licensed food production:
- Check state health department requirements before selling
- Most states require licensed commercial kitchen for products sold through retail channels
- Direct-to-consumer (farmer's market, farm stand) has different requirements by state

## Honest Representation vs. Health Claims

The protocol's complete mechanistic framework can be shared with customers in educational contexts (website, articles, books, one-on-one conversations) without violating the labeling prohibition. The label itself contains no health claims; the education happens elsewhere.
